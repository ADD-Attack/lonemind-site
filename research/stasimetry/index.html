LoneMind.ai — research note (short)

The question. Many AI and physics computations work by repeating a step until the answer stops changing. A light simulation does it, an equation solver does it, and so does a certain kind of neural network (deep equilibrium models). LoneMind.ai research studies how to certify those loops: a computable guarantee of how far the current answer is from the final one, and an honest refusal when no such guarantee exists. The programme's name is Stasimetry, "the measurement of coming to rest".

The core idea. If each pass shrinks the error by at least a factor q < 1, the distance to the final answer is at most q/(1−q) times the size of the last change (the classical Banach a-posteriori bound). The programme extends it in three ways:

  • Machine-checked proofs. The key lemmas are formalized in Lean and checked by its kernel, using only the standard axioms.
  • Sharpness. Each upper bound is paired with a "rest witness" that proves where no better guarantee is possible. This is inspired in part by the recent modular-bootstrap work, which pairs a bound with an optimality proof.
  • Honest refusals. Formal proofs pin down when a certificate cannot exist: volume-preserving dynamics, two competing rest states, or a step ratio drifting to 1. Weaker fallback guarantees fill those cases.

Early findings.

  • Equilibrium networks. Internal testing suggests the usual whole-model guarantee is very conservative. Certifying each answer locally tightens it roughly 40×, with zero failures against high-precision checks.
  • Classical gravity solvers (Jacobi and Gauss–Seidel for the Poisson equation). The usual "residual looks small" stopping rule often delivers several times less accuracy than requested. A certified stop delivers what it promises, every time in testing.
  • Black-hole photon rings. The textbook "each ring is about 1/23 of the last" approximation becomes an explicit inequality for the non-spinning case, with rigorous error terms.
  • A correction to a standard expansion. The 2002 strong-deflection expansion for black-hole lensing (Bozza) states a remainder of order ε. Internal analysis indicates it is actually of order ε·log(1/ε), and numerics support the extra log factor down to very small ε. The limiting ratio is unaffected.
  • The near-1 trap. When q sits extremely close to 1 (say 1 − 10⁻⁹), the computed step in double precision becomes exactly zero while the iterate is still measurably far from the fixed point. A naive certificate would then report zero error, which is false. Any certificate has to carry an explicit arithmetic-floor term, a point that matters for every AI system that iterates to equilibrium.
  • Mirror and saddle certificates. A loop that expands can still be certified by running it backwards, since an expansion is a contraction of the inverse. A mixed loop, stretching some directions and shrinking others, can be certified by splitting it: settle the shrinking part forward and the stretching part backward. In testing the split gave a certified rate of about 0.56–0.60 where both plain directions refused. Along the way the Lean kernel caught that the naive form of the backward bound is false at the starting point. It holds only from the next iterate on, and the formal version records that.
  • Wormholes. For short-throat traversable wormholes, successive lensed images shrink geometrically, so a certificate applies. For a long throat the step ratio drifts to 1. A formal proof then shows no geometric certificate can exist, and a slower 1/n² guarantee takes its place.

Status. Internal research, not peer-reviewed. Open to discussion.
