Whoa.
Everything wants to sit still.

Lots of AI and physics math does one thing, over and over, until the answer stops moving.

This page is about knowing, for sure, how far it still has to go.

toys below ↓

01

Roll a marble into a bowl.

Take a step. Take another. Each one a little smaller. Then: stillness.

A light simulation works like that. So does an equation solver. So does a certain kind of neural network (deep equilibrium models).

The big question: how far is the marble from the bottom right now? Not a guess. A guarantee.

The research has a name: Stasimetry. "The measurement of coming to rest."

Yes, someone named the act of sitting still. Respect.

The research adds three upgrades.

  • Machine-checked. The key lemmas are proved in Lean, and its kernel checks every step, using only the standard axioms. It never gets bored, and it never lets anything slide.
  • Can't beat it. Each guarantee comes with a "rest witness": a proof of where no better guarantee is possible. Part of the inspiration: recent modular-bootstrap work, which pairs a bound with an optimality proof.
  • An honest "nope." Sometimes a guarantee cannot exist, and formal proofs pin down when: volume-preserving dynamics (flip the toy to "No friction"), two competing rest states (one marble, two bowls), or a step ratio drifting to 1. Weaker backup guarantees cover those cases.

02

Shrink the bubble 40×.

Deep equilibrium networks usually get one guarantee for the whole model. Internal testing suggests it is very conservative.

Like saying your keys are "somewhere in this building." joke

Check each answer on its own instead, and the guarantee gets roughly 40× tighter. Zero failures against high-precision checks.

Meanwhile, in gravity math. Classic gravity solvers (Jacobi and Gauss–Seidel, for the Poisson equation) usually stop when the residual (how badly the current answer misses the equation) "looks small."

Plot twist: that rule often delivers several times less accuracy than requested. A certified stop delivers what it promises, every time in testing.

03

Rings inside rings inside rings.

Light skimming a black hole can loop around it before escaping, like water circling a drain. Every extra loop draws another thin ring. Picture a hallway of mirrors, bent into a circle.

The textbook approximation: each ring is about 1/23 of the last.

The research turns that "about" into an explicit inequality for the non-spinning case, with rigorous error terms. A proven fence around "about."

Plot twist number two. A 2002 formula for black-hole lensing (Bozza's strong-deflection expansion) says its leftover error is of order ε.

Internal analysis indicates it is actually of order ε·log(1/ε). If that holds up, a sneaky little log belongs in a formula from 2002. Numerics support the extra log factor down to very small ε. The limiting ratio is unaffected.

04

Run time backwards.

Some loops don't settle. They blow up, like a spring being stretched further and further.

The trick: rewind. A stretch, run backwards, is a squish. (The math version: an expansion is a contraction of the inverse.) So a loop that expands can still get a guarantee, in reverse.

Now the wild one: a loop that stretches one way and squishes the other. A saddle. Run it forward, it escapes sideways. Run it backward, it escapes up and down.

So split it. Settle the squishy part forward. Settle the stretchy part backward. Two directions of time, one answer.

The receipts. In testing, the split gave a certified rate of about 0.56–0.60, where both plain directions refused.

Plot twist number three. Along the way, the Lean kernel caught that the obvious version of the backward bound is false at the starting point. It only holds from the next step on, and the formal version records that. The proof checker was right.

05

The calculator that falls asleep near 1.

Push q super close to 1, like 1 − 10⁻⁹. Each step gets so tiny that double-precision arithmetic rounds it to exactly zero.

A naive certificate sees a zero step and says: zero error, all done.

But the answer is still measurably far away. That certificate is false.

So any honest certificate carries an explicit arithmetic-floor term. This matters for every AI system that iterates to equilibrium.

06

Wormholes, briefly.

Short-throat traversable wormhole: each lensed image shrinks by a steady factor. A certificate applies.

Long throat: the step ratio drifts toward 1. A formal proof shows no geometric certificate can exist there. A slower 1/n² guarantee takes its place.

The slow lane. Still a real lane.

So, yeah. Things come to rest.
Now there's a way to prove it.
