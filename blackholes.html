01 · Sum over histories

One particle, every route.

Send particles at a wall with two narrow slits, one at a time. Each lands as a single dot. After many dots, stripes appear, and stripes are what waves make when they overlap.[1]

Physics predicts this by adding up every route the particle could take. Each possible route is called a history, and some count for more than others.[1]

A quantum theory of gravity would have to go further and add up possible shapes of spacetime. Nobody yet knows how to do that in general.[1]

Try this: wait for the stripes, then cover one slit. They vanish.

Illustration. Toy pattern, not a simulated experiment.

02 · Anti-de Sitter space

A universe shaped like a bowl.

Empty space carries an energy, set by the cosmological constant. In our universe it is positive, and space expands faster and faster.[1]

Make it negative and you get anti-de Sitter space. It acts like a bowl: whatever you throw comes back. That also traps heat, so the universe can sit at a steady temperature, which makes it a clean place to study black holes.[1][2]

Try this: drag the energy above zero. The bowl flips and the ball never returns.

Illustration. A ball on a curve, not a solution of Einstein's equations.

03 · Holography

The rim knows what's inside.

Black holes store information in proportion to their surface area, not their volume, as Bekenstein and Hawking found in the 1970s.[1]

In 1997, Juan Maldacena made a sharper proposal: gravity inside the bowl is the same physics as a quantum theory with no gravity, living on the bowl's rim. One reality, two languages.[1]

That is useful because a question too hard in one language can be easier in the other.

Try this: move the object toward the rim. Its pattern sharpens, and the ring still finds it.

Illustration. The ring is computed from the rim bars alone, using a simple circle formula. It is an analogy, not the real duality.

04 · The Hawking-Page transition

Heat the bowl and two histories compete.

In 1983, Hawking and Page compared two versions of a hot bowl universe: one full of gas, one holding a black hole. Whichever counts more in the sum over histories is what you would see.[1]

Cold, the gas wins. Hot, the black hole wins. The handover is abrupt, like water boiling.[1]

For a five-dimensional bowl of radius R, black holes first exist at T = √2/(πR) ≈ 0.450/R, and take over at T = 3/(2πR) ≈ 0.477/R.[2] Lower on the graph means winning.

Try this: drag temperature slowly across 0.477 and watch the winner swap.

Illustration. Curve shapes and heights are schematic. They are drawn to cross at the two published temperatures.

05 · Result one · weak coupling

Abrupt or smooth? One number decides.

Hawking and Page's method needs a big, smooth universe. At the opposite extreme, spacetime goes blurry and only the rim theory (called N=4 super Yang-Mills) can be calculated. It has a transition there too, but was it abrupt or smooth?[1]

In 2003, physicists showed the answer hangs on the sign of one number, b.[2][4] Negative means a sudden jump. Positive means a gradual change.

The preprint finds b = −1.48 × 10⁻³ λ², where λ measures interaction strength. It is negative, so the transition is abrupt.[2]

The result covers weak coupling only. Staying abrupt all the way to strong coupling is the natural guess, but it is not shown.[1][2]

Try this: let it heat and cool. The ball jumps at different points each way, like superheated water.

Illustration. Uses the preprint's landscape formula, with |b| exaggerated to 0.1 as in the preprint's own figure.[2] The real |b| is far smaller. The slider shows the mass term m², which falls as temperature rises.

06 · Result two · the modular bootstrap

A ceiling that can't go lower.

Take a simpler universe, with two space dimensions. Nobody can write down its full quantum gravity, so physicists work by elimination: rule out every theory that contradicts itself.[1]

On a donut-shaped spacetime, either loop can be called “time”. Both choices must give the same answer, and that forces a ceiling on the lightest new state: at most c/Λ, where c roughly counts the universe's building blocks.[1][3]

A bigger Λ is a lower ceiling. The preprint finds the best this simplest, spin-ignoring version can do at large c:[3]

Λ = 12π² / (π² + 4 arcsinh²1) ≈ 9.1266

Black holes begin at c/12, so a gap remains. This method alone cannot close it; that will take other ingredients.[1][3]

Try this: step to the last rung. The shaded band is what this method can never rule out.

Illustration. Ceiling heights are the published values, using the leading c/Λ term only. The dots are made-up candidates, not real theories.

Precise scope: the optimum holds for a broad class of test functions that includes every finite-order one, as c grows large. Reaching it relies partly on computer-certified inequalities. The preprint states its ceiling for the “lightest primary”; the post describes the same quantity as the mass of the lightest black hole.[1][3]

07 · How the agents did it

A small research group of copies.

This is the account in Anthropic's post.[1] In August, researcher Hongbin Chen asked Claude for open problems. It returned 22 candidates. Separate agents took each one and ran in the background for about six weeks, able to read each other's progress.

Independent checks did much of the work. The weak-coupling preprint says almost every number was obtained two independent ways, and names the one exception.[2] The post says the results were formally verified in Lean, a computerized proof checker, in September, and that a physicist checked every main result.[1]
